Every Florida auction question that actually costs money reduces to one: **after the hammer falls, what is still attached to this property?**

A tax deed or a clerk's sale does not hand you a clean slate. It hands you a deed and a set of survivors. The survivors are what turn a $30,000 win into a $52,000 problem. This page is how to think about them before you bid, not after.

Nothing here is legal advice. Lien priority is decided by statute and by the specific documents recorded against one parcel, and the exceptions have exceptions. Read this to know what to look for and what to ask — then have a licensed Florida attorney read the actual title before you commit money.

## The base rule: first in time, first in right

Florida is a **race-notice** state. In broad terms, a properly recorded interest generally takes priority over interests recorded later, and a later buyer who records first without notice of an earlier unrecorded interest can come out ahead of it.

Practically, that means recording order is the starting point. Pull the chain, list every recorded encumbrance with its recording date, and you have the skeleton of a priority analysis.

It is only the skeleton. Three things bend it, and one thing breaks it entirely.

## What is being foreclosed decides what dies

This is the single most important distinction, and it is the one newcomers skip.

**A mortgage foreclosure forecloses the mortgage.** The sale extinguishes interests **junior** to the lien being foreclosed and leaves interests **senior** to it in place. If a second mortgage forecloses, the first mortgage does not vanish — the winning bidder takes title subject to it. People have paid five figures at a clerk's sale and inherited a six-figure first.

So the question at a foreclosure sale is never "what liens are on this?" It is "**where in the stack is the lien being foreclosed, and what sits above it?**" The case number tells you which lien is foreclosing; the recorded chain tells you what is senior to it.

**A tax deed sale is different in kind.** Florida's ad valorem tax lien sits at the top of the stack by statute rather than by recording date, so a tax deed generally extinguishes most private encumbrances recorded against the property — including mortgages that would otherwise be untouchable. That is the **wipe rule**, and it is why tax deeds attract investors: a property can arrive at the block carrying a mortgage many times the opening bid, and that mortgage is generally not the buyer's problem.

"Generally" is load-bearing. Certain governmental liens, easements, and restrictions of record are treated differently and can survive a tax deed. Which ones, and on what terms, is exactly the question to put to a Florida attorney for the specific parcel — it is not a thing to infer from a rule of thumb.

## The four ways priority surprises people

**1. Statutory priority that ignores recording dates.** Ad valorem property taxes are the clearest case: they outrank earlier-recorded private liens. Certain municipal and county obligations — code enforcement liens, utility and special assessments — are also treated specially in ways that vary by jurisdiction and by the ordinance behind them. A lien recorded last year can outrank a mortgage from a decade ago because a statute says so.

**2. Subordination.** Two lenders can agree in a recorded instrument that the earlier lien steps behind the later one. Recording order then tells you the opposite of the truth. Subordination agreements are recorded documents — they are findable, but only if you read the chain rather than skim a lien list.

**3. Omitted parties.** A foreclosure only extinguishes the interests of parties who were properly joined and served. Miss a junior lienholder in the complaint, and that lien survives a sale that should have wiped it. This is not rare. It is one of the strongest arguments for reading the docket, not just the lien list: who was named, who was served, who defaulted.

**4. Federal tax liens.** A recorded IRS lien is its own category. Federal law gives the United States a right of redemption for a period after certain judicial sales — commonly cited as **120 days** — during which the government can redeem the property from the purchaser. The lien position and the redemption right are separate questions, and both need checking when an IRS lien appears in the chain.

## Things that usually survive either sale

Some interests are not really "liens" in the sense that gets wiped, and they tend to follow the land:

- **Easements of record** — utility, drainage, access. A tax deed does not move a power line.
- **Restrictive covenants and plat restrictions** — what you may build and how you may use it.
- **Government police-power obligations** — an open permit or an unresolved code violation is frequently a condition of the property rather than a debt of the former owner, and the new owner inherits the problem even where the recorded lien amount does not survive.

The last one catches people who did the title work and skipped the county. Call code enforcement and the building department for the parcel before you bid.

## A workable pre-bid sequence

1. **Identify the foreclosing lien.** Tax deed, or a mortgage or association foreclosure? If a foreclosure, which position?
2. **Pull the recorded chain** and list every encumbrance with dates and amounts.
3. **Mark the statutory jumpers** — taxes, assessments, municipal liens — and do not assume their recording date reflects their rank.
4. **Read the docket, not just the chain.** Who was joined and served? An unjoined junior lienholder survives.
5. **Flag any federal tax lien** and treat the redemption window as a real risk to the timeline, not a footnote.
6. **Call the county** for open permits and code violations.
7. **Price the survivors into the ceiling** before you set a max bid. A lien you decide to pay is just a cost; a lien you discover afterwards is a loss.

Step 7 is the whole point. Priority analysis is not an academic exercise — it is an input to one number.
