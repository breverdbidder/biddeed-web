Florida is a **judicial foreclosure** state: a lender (or HOA, condo association, or other lienholder) cannot take a property without suing for it. The whole case is public record, which is why foreclosure investing is fundamentally a research business.

## The timeline, step by step

1. **Default.** The borrower stops paying. The lender sends notices and, for most residential mortgages, must wait out a required breach-letter period before filing.
2. **Complaint and lis pendens.** The lender files a foreclosure complaint in circuit court and records a lis pendens — the public notice that a case is pending. This is the earliest reliable signal a property is heading toward a sale.
3. **Service and answers.** The borrower and junior lienholders are served and can respond. Contested cases take longer; uncontested ones move.
4. **Judgment.** The court enters a final judgment of foreclosure, fixing the total owed and scheduling the sale — typically 20 to 35 days after judgment.
5. **Notice of sale.** The clerk publishes the sale date and terms. The property now appears on auction calendars — including BidDeed's.
6. **The auction.** Conducted by the clerk, online in most Florida counties (many use RealForeclose.com). The plaintiff usually opens with a credit bid up to the judgment amount; third parties bid above it.
7. **Certificate of sale, objections, certificate of title.** The winner gets a certificate of sale. There is a short window (typically ten days) for objections. If none hold, the clerk issues the certificate of title — that is when ownership transfers. The owner's right of redemption ends at the filing of the certificate of sale.

<SaleTypeCallout type="foreclosure" title="Who is foreclosing decides what survives">
  A first-mortgage foreclosure generally wipes junior liens behind it — but anything senior to the foreclosing lien survives. The classic trap: an HOA or condo foreclosure where the first mortgage stays attached to the property. A "cheap" condo at an HOA sale can come with the entire original mortgage still owed.
</SaleTypeCallout>

## Foreclosure vs tax deed: two different games

| | Foreclosure auction | Tax deed sale |
|---|---|---|
| Trigger | Unpaid mortgage or association dues | Unpaid property taxes |
| Seller | Court, via the clerk | County, via the clerk |
| What is being enforced | A private lien | The government's tax lien (senior to nearly everything) |
| Lien wipe | Junior liens wiped; **senior liens survive** | Junior liens generally wiped — including mortgages |
| Opening bid | Judgment-driven | Taxes, interest and costs (plus half assessed value if homestead) |
| Typical price vs value | Discount varies widely | Often the steeper discount, with steeper diligence risk |

The strategy difference follows from the lien math. At a foreclosure sale, the first question is "who is foreclosing, and what is senior to them?" At a tax deed sale, the first question is "what survives the wipe — government liens, IRS redemption, notice defects?" Both questions are answerable from public records before the sale. Both are covered in Tax Deeds 101's due-diligence checklist, and both are exactly what the SIGNAL$ report is built to answer per property.

## Where new bidders get hurt

- **Bidding at an HOA or condo foreclosure without checking for a senior mortgage.** The mortgage survives; your bargain is a lien, not a home.
- **Confusing the certificate of sale with ownership.** Until the certificate of title issues, the sale can unravel on objection.
- **Ignoring second auctions.** Sales get reset, canceled and rescheduled — the docket on sale morning is the only version that counts. (BidDeed's alerts exist for this.)
- **Pricing from the judgment instead of the market.** The judgment tells you what was owed, not what the property is worth. Your ceiling comes from the exit, same as always.
